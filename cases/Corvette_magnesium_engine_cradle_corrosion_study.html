Possibly relevant — automotive magnesium (15–20 lb/vehicle) placed under the dashboard where white rust is hidden. Not named explicitly; tagged speculatively.

Helicopters often use cast aluminum, cast magnesium parts. Today in automobiles there's about 15 or 20 pounds of magnesium in an automobile, mostly underneath the dashboard where it's protected from any road salt or anything like that. So it's basically in the occupant compartment, underneath the dashboard, so if it does start to show white rust, people don't see it. They also use magnesium sheet in some applications. So castings or sheet metal.

SMS_F2013_13 · Structural Materials Selection, Fall 2013 · §3.p2

Powell's counter to the conventional wisdom that magnesium corrodes badly. Cradle is right next to the engine, getting salt water from the road, but hasn't failed since 2006. Part consolidation: ~10 steel stampings replaced by a single magnesium die-casting.

For example, in the liftgate application, the secondary savings is that with a lighter liftgate you can use a smaller and cheaper motor. The most common application is the instrument panel beam that runs across the dashboard, holds the steering wheel instruments, glove compartment, etc. In steel that's about 30 different steel stampings spot-welded together. In magnesium it's one die-casting. The Corvette engine cradle — Corvette is a sports car so it's a little more weight-sensitive; it's had a fiberglass body for a long time for that reason — again, you go from about 10 steel stampings to a single magnesium die-casting. I like talking about this because magnesium's problems have generally been in corrosion and creep. But here it is in an application right next to the engine, getting salt water thrown at it from the road, and it doesn't corrode. Engines aren't falling out of Corvettes since 2006. It works quite well.
