New Orleans Canal Levee Failure

Appears in 4 lectures.

Appearances across the corpus

CS_Su2012_06 · Codes and Standards, Summer 2012 · §2.p1

Levee was designed to a safety factor of 1.3 and failed — illustrates that a "30 percent margin" is not actually a safety margin in practice when soil conditions and design-life uncertainties are factored in. Counter-example to the assumption that any nominal factor above unity is safe.

We have a big range of numbers. The failure of the canal levee in New Orleans was very unfortunate, and when they went back to the design — safety factor of 1.3. It turns out that's what they designed it for, and it wasn't enough. When Professor Eagar talked about learning from disaster, that's an example. Even though you were supposed to have that thirty percent margin — and it's big, it's huge — it's relatively easy to analyze and inspect. There were issues with the soil, there were a lot of things going on that were hard to predict 50 or 100 years before. That's why in the end they didn't have the capacity they thought they had.

WIE_F2015_11 · How to be a Successful Engineer, Fall 2015 · §8.p8

Reported post-Katrina safety factor of 1.3 — the lowest Tom knows of in a structural application. Personal connection: Tom's great-grandfather, a civil engineer, worked on the levees around 1880.

The New Orleans levees — around 1880 my great-grandfather was a civil engineer working on the levees — I read somewhere after Katrina that the safety factor on the levees was 1.3. That 1.3 is one of the lowest safety factors I know of. Typically safety factors are around 1.67 to 2. When you're hoisting equipment above someone's head, or someone's more than six feet off the ground, they'll go to 4. Some cases when you're working with castings and pressure vessels where the consequences of failure are pretty severe — blowing up a building or a city block — safety factor of five.

CS_F2012_12 · Codes and Standards, Fall 2012 · §5.p2

Safety factor 1.3. Tom emphasizes the levees did not fail by strength — they were overtopped. Personal note: Tom's grandfather built levees in New Orleans in the 1880s.

The New Orleans levees — someone may have mentioned that, because I pointed it out. Did he tell you what the safety factor on the levees is? It's 1.3. And frankly the levees didn't fail in New Orleans because the safety factor wasn't strong enough. They got breached over — they got washed away from the top. My grandfather in the 1880s was building levees in New Orleans.

CS_F2012_07 · Codes and Standards, Fall 2012 · §5.p1

A 1.3 safety factor design where the actual shear strength of saturated soil was the unknown — the failure was a material-property estimation error, not a safety-factor-too-low error.

So we're back here with respect to the different industries. I'm sure Professor Eagar started to introduce some of these values to you. If you have a levee — the New Orleans levee that came down with Katrina — it was designed with a safety factor of 1.3 because there's only so much dirt you can put. The force exerted by the water growing on top is very well known. It's just gravity times the height. The less understood aspect of this was the soil properties over time. So people made some assumptions a long time ago as to how much shear the soil was going to be able to take. But in terms of a full-scale assembly, it's very hard to do that test. So there's a lot of uncertainty as to what exactly a full-sized structure levee would be able to resist in terms of shear.
