Bombardier aircraft lightning testing - Canadian border incident

CS_Su2012_06 · Codes and Standards, Summer 2012 · §2.p3

The lecturer worked at Bombardier; uses landing-gear inspection and 4340 steel material predictability to illustrate why aerospace can run at 1.5 safety factor. *Note:* the canonical cluster name references a different Bombardier case (lightning testing); this lecture's reference is to landing gear design culture. Proposed sub-case or canon refinement: "Bombardier landing gear safety factor practice."

Aircraft structures — I put a 1.5 safety factor, because 1.6 maybe would be the one I've seen most. I worked for a bit at Bombardier Aerospace; they make small airframes. At the end of the day you go to a lower safety factor around the landing gear. It's typically very well inspected, very well determined. They have sensors everywhere to know exactly what the load history is for the landing gear, and therefore they can afford a lower safety factor. You save a tremendous amount of weight. And in a very controlled condition, you reduce the risk for something completely unusual to happen. If you have regular inspections you're more likely to pick up on small cracks that will grow, than if you say I'm going to build it strong enough and it's not going to be accessible for inspection, and we hope everything is okay for 20 years. That happens — in some small aircraft structures you can't really see inside the wings, so they don't really get inspected. And then all of a sudden you have cracks six inches, eight inches long, that were not predicted by design because of twisting modes that are harder to evaluate.

SMS_S2016_04 · Structural Materials Selection, Spring 2016 · §5.p3

Local-employment externality. Pittsfield MA lightning lab's technicians were barred at the Canadian border; engineers had to perform technician work in-country.

That's true in Canada, right next to us. I had an opportunity to go do some studies at a lab in western Pennsylvania that does lightning tests. I'd actually scheduled a time, and when I got there, there were nothing but technicians. That was because the engineers and some technicians were supposed to go to Canada to do some tests for Bombardier on the aircraft up there. When they got to the Canadian border, the Canadians looked at it and said: well, we'll let these engineers in because they have specialized skills to do these tests over the next three weeks in Canada, but we're not going to let your technicians in. We have technicians in Canada — they'll do all the technical work. So they had to send all their engineers to Canada to do technician work, because just because they had technicians in Canada didn't mean those technicians could do what these guys needed to do. But I got to go, and I got nothing but technicians in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, which turned out to be good because they told me all kinds of stories. That's another story.
