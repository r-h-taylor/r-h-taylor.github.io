Rubber-sealed pressure vessel crack-bridging failure

PROPOSED case — pending reconciliation against canon

Appears in 1 lecture.

Appearances across the corpus

CS_F2012_10 · Codes and Standards, Fall 2012 · §2.p1

A pressure vessel designed with factor-of-safety four; an internal rubber liner intended to protect the bottom welds and reduce corrosion exposure ended up bridging a circumferential crack so that it did not leak. The crack grew all the way around before the operators knew. Used to introduce leak-before-break as a design philosophy and to illustrate the limits of in-service visual inspection.

Another example of things that are not accounted for in the design: a pressure vessel with a factor of safety of four. On the inside, they had a rubber seal in the bottom — the rubber was at the bottom, working its way up a certain amount. We don't know the intent from the designer, but one advantage of doing this is you can reduce the exposure to oxygen. You can reduce the temperature of exposure for the metal. You can hide a welded connection that may not be as corrosion resistant as the cylinder itself. And it's the region where you have the most hydrostatic pressure, just from gravity.
