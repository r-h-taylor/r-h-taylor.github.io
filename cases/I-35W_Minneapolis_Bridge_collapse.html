CS_Su2012_06 · Codes and Standards, Summer 2012 · §4.p1

Fully developed teaching case. The lecturer was personally involved in the investigation (with the original design team). Teaches: (1) URS fatigue review covered members but not joints, (2) the original gusset plate was under-designed per the era's rules (NTSB finding), (3) added lanes, added pavement, corrosion, and stuck rollers consumed any residual margin, (4) post-collapse the inspectors' training curriculum added a chapter on gusset plate evaluation.

We'll talk a little bit about the bridge collapse in Minneapolis, which happened in 2007. It wasn't at rush-hour traffic but just before it — late in the afternoon. It was a very hot day. If you're thinking about a steel structure, a very hot day means thermal expansion. It probably had something to do with it. There was a lot of dispute, because this collapse was investigated first by NTSB, then MnDOT took over, and then experts for the different parties involved also got involved. The bridge to begin with had only two main trusses, one on each side, connected in such a way that you fail one of these connections and the bridge is gone. Everybody's analysis proved that.

CS_F2012_10 · Codes and Standards, Fall 2012 · §5.p1

The lecturer's own consulting work on the collapse. Used to illustrate (a) how factor of safety degrades over decades when maintenance assumptions are violated (locked bearings), (b) the dead-load fraction of bridge loading (80–85%), and (c) the coincidence of construction load, thermal expansion, and design omission that drove the failure. NTSB findings cited.

I worked on the bridge collapse in Minneapolis. So I'm only talking about it in a very general sense. You can think of somebody saying, "the bearings to hold the piers are locked." We talked about expansion in the first class, going back and forth. A bridge is a good example. The bridge is going to want to move if it's a metallic bridge between seasons. Now if the inspector says, "I think the bearings are locked, it's been there for 40 years," it kind of makes sense, and doesn't necessarily make a whole lot out of it. They don't say, "we've got to bring the firefighters to throw some grease in here and make sure that it starts moving again." Then your factor of safety is going down. Is it going down ten percent, twenty percent? Our margin has become lower already. And depending on whether you assume there was going to be maintenance that day, 40 years down the road, it makes a difference. Obviously you hope that within 40 years, people would go back and look at your design and try to figure out whether you were conservative or not. But it's not always the case.
