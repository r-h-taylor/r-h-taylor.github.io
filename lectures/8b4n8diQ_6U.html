§6. LIGO and the most precise measurement ever made [27:28]

§6.p1

If you're dealing in parts per million, you have to worry about these things. But who's dealing in parts per million? Well, some people are doing better than that. The most precise measurement ever attempted is in Livingston, Louisiana, and Hanford, Washington. Anyone from either the state of Washington or Louisiana ever heard about this? It's LIGO, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory.

◆
§6.p2

The LIGO project was originally funded at $300 million by the National Science Foundation. It was a joint project between MIT and Caltech. The chief scientist of LIGO is Ray Weiss, a retired professor of physics here. It's two Michelson interferometers. Anybody know what a Michelson interferometer is? You didn't learn this in freshman physics? Let's see if you can recall it. A Michelson interferometer — you can have a bench-type interferometer, but basically you have light coming in, you split it with a mirror, one beam goes down some length L1, the other typically at 90° goes down length L2, you have a mirror at the end of each, and the beams reflect back. As the wavelengths beat together, you can measure the phase angle difference between them, and you can tell the difference between L1 and L2 very precisely, because now you're measuring the shift in phase angle. It's the same beam of light, and the speed of light is a constant, so far as we know. So you can measure these two lengths very precisely because now it's a fraction of a wavelength of light. A Michelson interferometer is a very precise way to measure length, or the speed of light. If you know the length, you can measure the speed; if you know the speed, you can measure the length.

◆
§6.p3

LIGO has one of these in Livingston, Louisiana, and another in Hanford, Washington, a couple thousand miles apart. The length is 4 kilometers each. ◆
§6.p4

◆
§6.p5

In order to run this thing, they had to align the mirror at one end with the mirror at the other within 1 cm over 4 kilometers. One centimeter over 4 km is one part in 400,000. That's pretty precise. §7. Triple point of water and the temperature standard [36:27]

§7.p1

For temperature, what's the triple point of water or any pure substance? Where all three phases exist. The triple point is where solid, liquid, and vapor coexist. It is an invariant point thermodynamically — if you believe thermodynamics, and who even understands it, so how can you believe it? If I look at the triple point of water, there's pressure and temperature, and I have something that looks like this, where this is vapor, liquid, and solid, and there's a triple point. According to Gibbs, the phase rule says that's an invariant point. If I can come up with some way to get solid, liquid, and vapor to coexist, that temperature is uniquely defined. In fact the temperature scale is defined as the triple point of water is 273.16 degrees Kelvin. What's the melting point of ice at one atmosphere? 273.15. The triple point varies from the one-atmosphere melting point by a hundredth of a degree.

◆
§7.p2

[Tom holds up a triple-point-of-water cell.] For $1,500 you can go and buy from Fluke these cells. These cells were probably developed by some national laboratory somewhere, but it's nothing more than borosilicate or quartz tubes — the quartz is more expensive — filled with water. But not just any water. This is multiple distilled water, super pure water — they've gotten rid of all the salts and stuff — because this water has to have the same isotopic concentration as Vienna mean sea water. People have measured lake water, rain water, ocean water, and they all have a different isotopic concentration of oxygen-16, oxygen-17, oxygen-18, hydrogen, deuterium, tritium. All of those will change the triple point depending on isotopic concentration. Because these things have accuracies of — let me just read it to you.

◆
§7.p3

The uncertainty is less than one millionth of a degree, or one ten-thousandth of a degree centigrade, for these things. So if you want to calibrate your thermometers, you can buy these little special cells of water. You can also buy them in gallium, ultra pure copper, and you can do a whole set of temperature scales if you want to be accurate within a fraction of a Kelvin. We don't usually need that, in most cases. But some people do, when they're trying to make very precise measurements such as the fundamental constants.
