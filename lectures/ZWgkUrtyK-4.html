§2. Why military and commercial jet engines diverged [03:10]

§2.p1

One of the problems — and this is really getting into a digression from corrosion — is about twenty or thirty years ago, development of military jet engines and development of commercial engines diverged. Anybody know why? Because they were looking for fuel efficiency in the commercial area. A fifty degree Fahrenheit increase in the operating temperature of a commercial jet engine will save just U.S. airlines fifty billion dollars a year in fuel. That's a lot of money. That's why they're always talking about fuel economy, and they retire engines and look for the latest engines which are more fuel efficient.

◆
§2.p2

In order to get high fuel efficiency, they decided to go to what's called the high bypass jet engine. So there's two equations: one-half mv squared, and the momentum equation, mv. The old jet engines, which are still the military engines, were just a long tube — whatever came in with a certain amount of mass went out the back with the same mass, only it had a much bigger velocity. So a lot more energy, and the energy came from burning the fuel. But the actual thrust-to-weight ratio is momentum. The problem with momentum is you didn't have a lot of mass going through this little tube. So they came up with a high bypass and put these big fans around it. You see this on a commercial jet — engines that are ten feet in diameter. The basic core engine is still in there, but the engine is now driving these fans, which are pulling another mass, a bigger mass. So you get lots of high velocity with a little m through the core, but you get lots of mass with a little v by the fans, and now you get tremendous thrust and tremendous efficiency.

◆
§2.p3

The problem is you can't do this with a supersonic engine, and the military engines want to go above the speed of sound. Commercial engines will go above the speed of sound, but the military engines still are basically the core. They're inherently fuel inefficient, because they want to break the sound barrier. What's the solution? It turns out you can't run modern engines without a computer. The computer is changing parameters in that engine a thousand times a second or faster. The airfoils that control a modern military aircraft — when you're going Mach 1 and a half and you want to take a right turn, no human could respond to all the control surfaces, so you have computers running those control surfaces. A lot of the plane designs now are inherently unstable, just like a submarine going through the water. We won't talk about the fact that the deeper you are, the slower you have to go, because if you go unstable and go deeper than you're supposed to go, it's not a good thing in a submarine.

◆
§2.p4

Same thing in a jet engine. If all of a sudden one of the control surfaces sends you in another direction you didn't want to go, it's not a good thing. When you're going Mach 1 and a half and you take a ninety degree turn, the g forces were such that the pilot will pass out. In fact that's one of the problems: the F-22 and the F-35 can do maneuvers that the pilot cannot take above about eight or nine g's. The blood doesn't get to the brain anymore. The heart is not strong enough to pump it. Because of that, the solution is drones — pilotless aircraft — which should be one of the solutions for the Navy to think of: ships without people.

§3. The cost of corrosion as a fraction of GNP [10:14]

§3.p1

I just happened to get this book — it arrived after class yesterday — on cathodic protection. I'm sure you all are busy purchasing books like this. It says that the first study on the cost of corrosion was done in 1976, and they found it cost the U.S. seventy billion dollars in 1976, somewhere between three and a half and four and a half percent of the country's gross national product. They redid the study in 1982 and it was a hundred twenty-six billion. By 1991 it was two hundred seventy-six billion. If you calculate that, it's higher than the inflation rate. What it is — lies, damn lies, and statistics — they were including other things. For example, they assume that every automobile that's junked is junked because of corrosion. That's not necessarily true. A lot of them are junked because they're totaled in a crash. That's not corrosion.

◆
§3.p2

However, they've done two studies, one by NIST and one by the National Association of Corrosion Engineers, and they both came up with corrosion costs about 4.2 to 4.9 percent, plus or minus thirty percent statistics, of the gross national product. If it is about five percent of the gross national product, what's that today? The gross national product is about fifteen trillion. So five percent of that is seven hundred fifty billion. Now do you believe that's the cost of corrosion? Neither do I. But that's what the National Association of Corrosion Engineers will tell you. They'll do whatever they do to inflate the numbers, because it's in their best interest. But corrosion does cost a lot of money. The main principle: metals don't want to be metals by nature, so they will go back to their original state over time. The way they do it is by corroding. Corrosion, if you look in the dictionary, is the chemical degradation — it's a slow chemical process. For a corrosion engineer, it's the degradation over time of metals.

§4. Three Mile Island: stress corrosion versus stress-assisted cracking [12:43]

§4.p1

Then you get into questions like Three Mile Island — the sister reactor at Three Mile Island, where they wanted to restart that nuclear reactor. They started up the steam generator. There's a type of acid that you can't produce regularly in the laboratory, but under the high pressures and the startup temperatures, with some sulfur contamination, you get polythionic acid. It's a polymerized sulfur compound. This nickel-based superalloy heat exchanger on reactor 2 — not the one that had all the problems, but they were trying to restart it so it could generate electricity a couple years after Three Mile Island — they started it up, and within a half an hour they had stress corrosion cracks, and it cost them thirty million dollars to repair.

◆
§4.p2

The fundamental question for the insurance carriers was: if all the damage was done in a half an hour, was that a slow process? Was that corrosion? The first press releases to come out of Three Mile Island, from GPU Nuclear which was the utility, the first day and a half they called it stress corrosion cracking, SCC. By the time the attorneys had gotten hold of it, they called it stress-assisted cracking, because the attorneys looked at their insurance contract which excluded corrosion — because corrosion is just a slow gradual wearing away. They knew that if they called it stress corrosion cracking, the insurance company was going to deny the thirty million claim. If they could change the name and call it stress-assisted cracking — well, what cracking is not stress-assisted? You start getting into the story about lawyers. You can go back to the Bible and see what the Bible says about lawyers. But in any case, that's what they do.

§5. Black iron pipe and the magnetite protective coating [14:47]

§5.p1

The second point from yesterday: metals may possess immunity, or we called it nobility, or they can have practical nobility, which means they've got a coating of some sort of protective oxide or something else. To give you an example of something that has a protective oxide — anybody know what this is? [Tom holds up a length of pipe.] Some of you must be mechanical engineers. You should call this a pipe nipple. It's threaded on both ends. Bought it at Home Depot, cost me like a buck. Three-quarter inch by six, black nipple. Why is it a black nipple? Because it's got a coating of Fe₃O₄, magnetite, on the surface. When you heat up steel to above about five or six hundred degrees Fahrenheit — and this thing gets to temperatures of eight or nine hundred degrees when they're forming it in the steel mill — it will form, not Fe₂O₃ which you know as rust, but Fe₃O₄ which is called magnetite. It's slightly magnetic. Ever play that little game with the magnets where you put the mustaches and the beards on the little figures? That's magnetite. That's another use for magnetite.

◆
§5.p2

Black iron pipe has its own inherent coating. If you go down to the basement of MIT and look up, you'll see the fire sprinkler system, and it's black iron pipe. You can tell it's a fire sprinkler system because the couplings are either orange or red — you've seen these Victaulic-type couplings that have gaskets. You probably have similar types of things on board ship. It's black iron pipe. They don't do anything other than just take the regular mill finish — although this one someone put varnish on, which doesn't hurt it. And that keeps it from corroding. How come rust is not protective, Fe₂O₃? Because it flakes off. Rust is flaky.

◆
§5.p3

If you can keep a protective coating on something, you can actually get pretty good protection. This comes out of one of these marine books that I showed you yesterday, and it shows what happens to the corrosion rate in millimeters per year versus exposure time in days. A year is right about in here. If you have attached rust, the corrosion rate is on the order of a tenth of a millimeter per year. What's that in inches? You're Canadian, you know the metric system. A millimeter is forty thousandths of an inch, so a tenth of a millimeter is four thousandths of an inch. I did that calculation in my head. That is the typical corrosion rate for steel in the atmosphere, or in lots of other humid or moist environments. This happens to be sea water, and it was probably painted — but where the paint was worn away, it did rust at about four thousandths of an inch per year. That is a good general rule of thumb for the corrosion rate of steel in water.

◆
§5.p4

The numbers I use are typically 0.002 to 0.010 inches per year — two to ten thousandths of an inch per year. Sometimes people come to me and say, oh, we had this piece of black iron pipe which is about an eighth of an inch thick and it corroded through in one year. And I said, no it didn't. It should last ten years even in a very severe ten-thousandths-an-inch environment, and at four thousandths of an inch per year it should last about twenty-five years. Those of you who have been aboard ship and you have carbon steel piping, how long does it last? About twenty-five or thirty years before you really need to get rid of the whole thing. That used to be the limiting lifetime on ships. But then someone at NAVSEA said, oh, we need ships that will last fifty years, because we can't afford to replace them so often.

§7. The history of polyethylene and the hula hoop [22:00]

§7.p1

So why haven't we switched to plastic? We have been switching to plastic. It turns out plastics are sort of a recent development. The history of polyethylene, which is one of our most common plastics and one of our cheapest plastics — polyethylene really didn't exist in World War Two. They knew about it but it wasn't a commercial development.

◆
§7.p2

In the 1950s, one of the big plastics companies tried to build a plant because they could. They decided they were going to be able to produce hundreds of millions of pounds of polyethylene every year. They started producing it, but they didn't really have a market. Their original manufacturing quality was variable. They tried to take it to somebody and say, why don't you use this for plastic pipe. Because it's easy to extrude, you form everything at temperatures you hardly burn yourself in the plant. They hadn't come down the learning curve of manufacturing to get consistent quality.

◆
§7.p3

§9. PEX and crosslinking [30:43]

§9.p1

Plastics do corrode, many times in very subtle ways. Some plastics will stress corrosion crack in very clean water, nearly pure water. Water can attack plastics. Same thing with ceramics. They often want to use ceramics at very high temperatures, like in some engine part. Well, sulfur compounds just destroy ceramics in general. These other things do corrode. Environmentally they degrade rather than corrode. But why don't they use PEX? PEX is cross-linked polyethylene, and there are two types of PEX. There's PEX-A and PEX-B. PEX-B is electron beam polymerized.

◆
§9.p2

Remember Charles Goodyear and sulfur and putting it in latex and making rubber? That's how he learned how to make rubber in the 1830s, by putting sulfur in with the latex, and the sulfur would create crosslinks between the long polymer chains. I'm not getting a lot of nods here of remembering Charles. Charles Goodyear got a patent on it, but that's because he didn't know that the ancient Mayans actually used a vine that had natural sulfur compounds. They would mix that, and so their rubber ball — they used to play this game over miles and miles with this rubber ball. Mike Tarkanian, who runs the lab over here, for his bachelor's thesis was the one who rediscovered the fact that the Mayans from thousands of years ago used the morning glory vine, a type of morning glory that had natural sulfur compounds. They mix it with the rubber and they get crosslinking.

◆
§9.p3

You've got these polymer chains, which in polyethylene is basically just C with a bunch of H's. So you got a backbone of carbon — it could be ten thousand carbons long — with hydrogen, and at the end you have to have another hydrogen. The formula is Cₙ H₂ₙ₊₂. For every carbon you got two hydrogens, and you have to have a hydrogen on the ends, both ends, so it's 2n plus 2. That's the formula for polyethylene. It has the exact same formula as paraffin wax. One way to tell if something is polyethylene is you take a little piece, you burn it, and see if it smells like a candle. This is actually one of the scientific tests for polyethylene. You need to understand these sophisticated tests we use in the world.

◆
§9.p4

PEX-A, they basically get crosslinking by hitting it with electron beam, which breaks some of these bonds, and you can actually get links — now you have a carbon coming off this way and this way. You can only have two carbons at a time, but you can have crosslinks with carbon, and you get the same type of thing as taking latex and vulcanizing it. They can do it with an electron beam, or they can do it chemically where they add compounds that help break down some of the polymer chains. Here they're just using a lot of energy to break the polymer chain and hope that it reforms, and it does. This is made by a company called Uponor, this is made by almost everybody else. Now you have enough strength in all directions, and these things don't just burst on you.

◆
§9.p5

The best anyone knows on the PEX, which has been in service for about twenty-five years now, it shows no evidence of deterioration. The PEX will probably last about a hundred years or more. That's the best estimate right now. People are complaining about the brass fittings, but we'll talk about that when we get to the alloying. In fact that's why people like it — you don't have to use much copper. I mentioned yesterday some people are tearing the copper out of their homes to sell the copper and putting PEX back in. So they're taking the investment value in their home, the copper pipes, and replacing it with PEX.

◆
§9.p6

The problem is, some attorneys in Las Vegas got about a quarter billion dollar settlement for corrosion of some of the brass fittings, and so now there's a feeding frenzy by attorneys claiming this causes leaks, except no one's ever seen one of the leaks. They do get corrosion in Las Vegas. Water is not the best water in the world. You can drink it, but it's not good for your pipes. That's another story.

Cases referenced

  • Pratt & Whitney military jet engine design defect lawsuit (1992) §1.p1

    Used to establish the 1992 Supreme Court ruling that the government, not the manufacturer, owns design liability for military hardware. Sets up the V-22 case that follows.

  • V-22 Osprey Ship Four crash and rigged court of inquiry §1.p2

    Tom worked on the case. Used to illustrate the military/civilian liability split: Boeing and Bell settled with military families immediately; civilian families had to file suit and go to trial.

  • Three Mile Island §4.p1

    The *sister* reactor (reactor 2) to the famous accident. Restart attempt produced polythionic acid attack on a nickel-based superalloy heat exchanger; stress corrosion cracking in thirty minutes; $30 million repair. Used as the worked example of the legal distinction between "stress corrosion cracking" (insurance-excluded) and "stress-assisted cracking" (insurance-covered) — and as a case study in how attorneys rename phenomena to fit contract language.

  • Stress corrosion cracking terminology insurance dispute §4.p2

    Tom presents the GPU Nuclear / attorney renaming of "stress corrosion cracking" as "stress-assisted cracking" specifically to defeat the insurance policy's corrosion exclusion. The teaching point: terminology is contract language as much as it is physics.

  • US corrosion cost study and Congress funding §3.p1

    The 1976 → 1982 → 1991 → ~2014 series of cost-of-corrosion studies (NIST and NACE). Tom uses the escalating numbers to teach skepticism about aggregate-statistic methodology ("lies, damn lies, and statistics") while granting the underlying point that corrosion is genuinely expensive.

  • Polyethylene manufacturing and hula hoop market development §7.p2

    1950s polyethylene overproduction problem solved by the hula hoop fad — fifty million pounds of variable-quality scrap polymer found its first mass market in a toy. Used to set up the substitution sequence (polyethylene's creep limit at ~150°F motivated polybutylene, which motivated PEX).

  • Polybutylene pipe and fitting failures §8.p1

    Plumbing material that replaced polyethylene; fittings susceptible to stress corrosion cracking in certain water chemistries; class action settlement.

  • Consultant's daughter polybutylene condo replacement lawsuit (Raleigh, NC) §8.p4

    Tom's personal anecdote — daughter bought a condo with polybutylene in 1998, joined the class action just under the wire, received $2,500 to replace fittings.

  • Delrin (polyformaldehyde) toilet valve failures and exploding toilets §8.p5

    The lecture's center case. DuPont's 1958 paper tested Delrin against 200 solvents — but never against water. 1962 German study found chlorine-induced stress corrosion cracking. Hoechst paid $900M settlement; DuPont paid more than $1B (confidential). Toilet float valves made of Delrin became brittle after 2–3 years and failed under 60 psi water pressure, blowing off ceramic tank tops.
