§5. World Trade Center, Lester Thurow, and the soundbite world [19:47]

§5.p1

I've given you some other handouts. One's an article I wrote a couple weeks after the World Trade Center collapsed, which for about eight or ten years was the number one article if you looked up Google "WTC collapse." The reason it was the number one article is I was asked by an editor to write it. I was so sick of hearing false information, like the steel melted in the fire. Anyone who's ever been to a fire knows that steel doesn't melt in a fire. If steel melted in the fire, we didn't need Sir Henry Bessemer in 1856 to teach us how to melt steel — we would have been melting it for a few thousand years before that. I wrote this in three hours, which is less time than it took for me to write any of my several hundred publications. I've gotten more comments on this, positive and negative, than any paper I've ever written, or actually all of the papers combined. Why? Because I wrote it for a high school science student. I was trying to write it so someone could understand it.

§5.p2

§5.p3

§5.p4

We live in a soundbite world.

◆
§5.p5

My favorite quote from Lester was a few years later. He was Dean of the Sloan School, giving a lecture at the Marriott Hotel to a group of alums. He was lamenting the fact that we tend to lose some of our best faculty to other universities who pay big bucks for them. Everyone kind of knew he was talking about Robert Merton. Merton was the guy who worked with Black and Scholes on derivatives — not dy/dx, but derivatives as trading on Wall Street. It's called the Black-Scholes method, but Merton was the postdoc working with these faculty, and they came up with this way to predict the price of a stock in the future in a very precise mathematical way. This led to the whole derivatives trading, which led to the collapse of the economy in 2008. You have a bunch of people on Wall Street sitting around the table placing bets with each other.

◆
§5.p6

They call it selling the risk, trading the risk. Every time someone who owns $5 billion worth of mortgages says, I'll sell you $3 billion of these, but you're going to have to give me 2%, or I'll give you 2% for assuming the risk — they just go around in a circle and keep trading paper, no value added. And everyone is getting a cut of the action on trillions of dollars. The amount of derivatives in 2004 in the world was $1 trillion. The amount of derivatives in 2008, at the time of the collapse, was $50 trillion. It's equal to the world's gross domestic product for a year. When a couple of banks defaulted, they were going to have to pay another bank, and they didn't have the cash. The other bank was going to have to pay the other guy on the other side of the table, and they didn't want to give up their cash. Just like the stock market crash of '29: everyone wanted to hold on to their cash, because they were afraid someone was going to call them on their paper.

◆
§5.p7

Merton had been a faculty member at Sloan. He got hired away for big bucks to Harvard, and a couple years later won the Nobel Prize. Lester's lamenting the fact that some of our star faculty are hired away, but he says: fortunately they tend to hire our extinct volcanoes. Think about it. He didn't have to explain to anybody in the room what he meant by an extinct volcano in that context. You need to learn to communicate in vivid metaphor. That's what the management at the top wants to hear, folks.

§6. Why steel: ceramics, toughness, and critical flaw size [27:02]

§6.p1

Another article you'll get is Materials Research Needs in the 21st Century. I wrote this for the National Research Council of the National Academy. They had a committee of distinguished people trying to tell the Defense Department what high-tech materials they would be using in the 21st century. Copyright 2003, so we were doing this around 2001. Harvey Schadler was chair, General Electric Research. Alan Liss, General Dynamics retired. James Baskerville, Bath Iron Works — I think he was a grad of this program too. Millard Firebaugh I mentioned; he was at Electric Boat then, but has been Chief Engineer of the Navy.

◆
§6.p2

I didn't really fit in, because I tended to agree with this million-dollar US Army study. The US Army had done a study in the late 1990s of what material they would be using the most of in the next 30 or 40 years. Anybody know what it was? Student: Aluminum? A million dollars to figure this out. Student: Humans? Steel. I'm going to tell you why it's steel in a moment. Because I could convince them that it would be steel, and they needed me to write something, I sort of took the outline of my material selection course — which is one of the modules of 12 units — and I put it into this four-page summary, which they put into an appendix. It doesn't have my name associated with it. I'm not ashamed of it.

◆
§6.p3

In 1992 I wrote this article in the Welding Journal called "The Future of Metals." This was at a time when people thought fine ceramics were going to take over jet engines and everything else, and they didn't. I was predicting in 1992 that they wouldn't. How did I know? Because I was looking at the properties. What do you think about a ceramic as a structural material? Student: Hard, brittle. Hard, brittle, right. High temperature too, which is why they wanted it for jet engines. They actually built jet engines, but it turns out the critical flaw size for a brittle material is extremely small. The formula is simple: the toughness has to be greater than the stress times the square root of pi times the crack length. You can get a very high applied stress. The toughness of a ceramic is terrible. Anyone ever seen someone cut glass? They score it. They take a carbide or a diamond tool and scratch the surface, and then they hit it with the heel of their hand and it'll break right where they put the scratch — because the critical flaw size is less than a human hair for a brittle material like glass.

◆
§6.p4

So as long as you make a jet engine with no imperfections larger than half or one-tenth the size of a human hair, it'll run at great temperatures. We've already done that. It's called a cruise missile. The cruise missile engine is made out of graphite. Graphite's not particularly tough, but it only has to last for an hour, and the bigger problem is keeping it from burning up in an hour. They put coatings on it. But graphite's tougher than most of the ceramics.

◆
§6.p5

When I was in Tokyo in the mid '80s, they had what they called ceramics fever. They had a ceramic show in 1985, when I left — a million people showed up. Not just scientists; everybody thought fine ceramics were great and were going to take over the world. I came back and gave talks at NIST in Gaithersburg, Maryland. I got up and said, the problem with ceramics is they don't have any toughness. The only structural materials made out of ceramics are Portland cement and toilet bowls. I'd throw in the kitchen sink, but you've got to line it with cast iron to give fracture toughness. If you drop something in the sink and it was just made out of ceramic, you'd end up with a shattered sink. So they make it out of cast iron — and gray cast iron is the worst metal of all in terms of toughness. People forget this toughness requirement.

§7.p1

◆
§7.p2

Here's the fracture toughness K₁c squared over pi times sigma s — basically the critical crack length. If this is in centimeters, 100 cm would be the critical flaw size. That's pretty big, except the only materials that will cross that line are some of the engineering alloys, and steels are right up here. You want the highest strength and the highest toughness, so up at 45° you want everything at the top of this, and that's where steels are, along with nickel, titanium, copper. Aluminum alloys are way down here. Engineered ceramics are down here — critical flaw size of 10⁻³ cm, less than a human hair. Ceramics are great as long as you load them in compression. That's what we do with Portland cement.

◆
§7.p3

Student: I don't know what it means to load something under compression — for instance that 3,000 psi, what does that mean? Okay. Portland cement: you start with limestone, put it in a kiln, drive the CO₂ off, make burnt lime, which is calcium oxide, magnesium oxide, and other things. You throw some sand in, which makes a composite, and you mix it with water. The water hydrates the burnt lime: calcium oxide plus H₂O gives you calcium hydroxide. This is a way oversimplified formula, but that's basically it. It takes time to set up, and it gives off heat. If you pour Portland cement and stick a thermometer in it, five hours later it'll be about 140 or 150°F. It's an exothermic reaction. It takes a month to get to its engineering strength.

◆
§7.p4

When they pour the foundation for the new NanoTech building, they'll pour cores and send them to a laboratory. They may test them after one week — it has to support a thousand psi after one week, because it hasn't finished setting up. After 30 days its structural design strength should be 3,000 psi, and if it fails, rip it out and start over. Student: That tells me you load it to make sure it's working correctly, to make sure there are no flaws. But you only put it in compression. If you want to put it in tension, you have to use steel rebar.

◆
§7.p5

[Tom produces a piece of steel rebar.] Here's a piece of steel rebar. It's got notches rolled into the surface, so that the concrete grabs those, and that takes the tension. If you make a concrete beam, the bottom of the beam that's in bending tension — it's the steel that's taking the tension, not the concrete. Concrete may have 500 psi tension capability, but it depends on the size of the bubbles in there. The engineering credit you get for concrete in tension in an engineering design is zero, and 3,000 psi in compression. When they were pouring some steps in the basement of NanoLab, they have wire mesh for the steps. Well, steps are mostly in compression — you stand on them, you're not pulling on them.

◆
§7.p6

§9. The Sea Wolf weld inspection problem [44:59]

§9.p1

One of the points I'm trying to make — this is one of the two or three for today's lecture — is that we use metals because they have good toughness, and we don't use ceramics because they have lousy toughness. Their critical flaw size is so small that you can never inspect for those things. The Sea Wolf submarine problem, to get back to a Navy example, is that they ended up getting too high carbon content and alloy content. The weld metal was 30 or 40% higher strength than it was intended to be, because of the chemistry, and they got a less tough steel. It formed little microscopic flaws that the original inspectors weren't looking for, something that small. It's a different type of cracking that we hadn't seen before. They had built 18% of the ship before a guy found it.

◆
§9.p2

He found it by a non-destructive test technique that was not exactly authorized. The outside of the hull has to be very smooth. They grind off the weld reinforcement, because you want a hull so smooth that even the weld reinforcement would create enough of a ripple that a guy listening on the other side could hear it. So they grind the weld smooth on the outside surface. The grinder was grinding, and he saw some of the grinding swarf — what's grinding swarf? Grinding swarf is the little powder you get from grinding. He noticed some of it had been magnetized — the steel had been magnetized, and the magnetic field was aligning with these little cracks in the weld metal. He had never seen that before. He had enough sense to call over an inspector — what's that, I've never seen that before. They went in with their instruments and found these things that were about a millimeter in size. Typically all the requirements for any inspection anywhere — except some of Boeing's, which are tighter than anything the world ever needed — typically we're looking for eighth of an inch, 3 mm flaws. These were 1 mm flaws, all over the place but very small.

◆
§9.p3

They had to cut out every weld in the sub. Student: What testing did they usually do? Typically magnetic particle, but they're looking for large flaws. If your minimum detectable flaw size is an eighth of an inch reliably detectable — the Air Force did a bunch of studies in the 1980s on what they call probability of detection. If you plot the probability of detection by flaw size in inches, you'll be at 95% probability of detection by a manual inspection at an eighth of an inch. You'll be at about 50% at a sixteenth of an inch. These were down in the millimeter size, very low probability of detection. Aircraft engines, they're looking for things 0.015". We don't really have inspection technologies that can do it, and Boeing has specs of 0.010". No one can find it, but it's in the spec.
