§3. Deformation processing and the languages of engineering [12:56]

§3.p1

Student: There's three courses that I'm guessing overlap — Material Processing, Material Processing in Practice, and Deformation Material Processing. Could you tell me the difference, what's in each, so I can decide which one I want?

◆
§3.p2

If you go to the website it actually gives you a brief synopsis of what's in each. Deformation processing is Tom Eagar teaching out of Al Backofen [Backofen]. Backofen was the professor who taught me as a sophomore, and he was a world's expert in how do you shape and form metals — whether it's forging or drawing or extrusion or wire drawing, how do you stamp car bodies. I used to teach his course 40 years ago. Then I taught it once as a module, and it's one of my favorite modules, because hey, I'm interested in the topic.

◆
§3.p3

It's everything that goes through the crystal structure — what we call the anisotropy — that allows you to do deeper draws of steel and aluminum, like a beverage can. It's actually called drawing and ironing, but it's a little bit different. It goes through all those solid metal forming processes — from the crystal structure through the forces involved in drawing, forging, wire drawing, any way you can think of, coining. How do you stamp a coin, and what forces are going to be involved.

◆
§3.p4

The other materials processing ones are probably ones that were done by Simone Belmar [Belmar?]. He basically gives a lot of examples from some of his consulting experience of how processing in general is important. He's more of a mechanical engineer doing this, so it's a more general approach, like you get in a mechanical engineering course. Mine are more in-depth into the materials aspects. For example, his process is not going to get into the crystal structure and how it changes things. That's a secondary effect, a very important secondary effect. Don Baskin will tell you that was critical in all the forming of automobile body sheets. It has been critical for 60 years. But generally a mechanical engineer won't worry about that.

◆
§3.p5

In fact, yesterday Don was talking about looking things up in a table. When I was lecturing live, I would bring in a piece of aluminum bar and I would say what's this? Some students would say well, it's metal. That's correct. Another student would say it looks like it's aluminum. That's correct. And if I was a metallurgist I would say it's 6061 T6 aluminum, which is a specific alloy and a specific heat treatment.

◆
§3.p6

Every engineering group has its own phraseology, its own semantics. Civil engineers use S for stress; mechanical engineers and materials engineers use sigma for stress. If you go to a civil engineering book you've got to learn the notation, because everybody uses things that are different. I used to call this learning to be multilingual. If I'm talking to an audience of a bunch of managers, I have to talk manager-ese, which is kind of slow school technology, and they would be happy with "it's a bar of aluminum." But if I'm talking to a bunch of metallurgists I'd better tell them it's 6061 T6, because they're going to know the difference and they're going to be concerned about the difference. And if I don't answer that question for them, their mind's going to be wondering about that and not paying attention to the rest of what I'm talking about.

§3.p7

You have to speak to your audience. You can't give the same lecture to everybody, although that's what I'm forced to do in teaching, which is one of the reasons I teach in parables, by telling you stories. So I'll tell you a story about consulting, since you brought it up.

§4. The consulting parable: the foreman knows [18:17]

§4.p1

Students would ask me how do you consult for a company. One of the problems in consulting with a company is that management called you in as a consultant because they had their own engineers who'd been looking at this problem for several weeks and can't solve it. So they're going to call someplace like MIT and find somebody who supposedly knows something. They're going to bring them down, pay them hundreds of dollars an hour. And the engineers who are making $75 an hour or whatever are not really all that happy about having someone come in. And the worst thing that expert can do is walk in and shoot from the cuff and give the answer in the first five minutes.

◆
§4.p2

So you go into a meeting when you get there as a consultant, and you've got the managers who want the problem solved but they don't really know what the problem is — that's why they brought you in — and their engineers can't explain it to them. They just start talking engineering, and it's like two people talking different foreign languages. The managers will introduce you to the engineers, the engineers will explain the problem. But they don't want to really explain everything, because if they think you're going to solve it quickly, it's going to make them look bad.

◆
§4.p3

So you listen to all this, and then you ask, well, can we go out in the plant and take a tour? They all say yeah, they're all ready for a break. Got to get a new cup of coffee. You go out in the plant and you're walking around, and you find the foreman, the hourly worker. You find a chance to talk to that guy. First you have to establish a little rapport with him about this pretty interesting machine he's working with here. And you ask him — or her — what the problem is, and they will tell you. They know what the problem is. The answer is right there in that company.

◆
§4.p4

But the managers and engineers don't respect the hourly worker who's been doing this for 40 years and knows exactly what the problem is, and has tried to tell them, and has gotten to the point where he refuses to tell them anything else. It's all a communications problem. So he tells you what it is, he tells you in his jargon, his language. You go back into the conference room, you translate it into manager-ese and engineering-ese for everyone else. You walk out having solved the problem. You didn't solve the problem, you didn't know what the problem was — all you did was help them. And they still don't even understand it afterwards. Even if you tell them the story, they don't understand. The answer was right there in their own factory, and they just didn't have the respect for each other to ask each other, and they didn't try to speak each other's language.

◆
§4.p5

It's mostly communications problems, folks. It's not technical problems.

§5. The donuts story: Honeywell New Jersey [21:37]

§5.p1

Have I told you the donuts story from Honeywell? For those of you that might be LGO students, this is in the early days of leadership manufacturing. LGO used to be LFM. Honeywell became part of the program, and Honeywell had bought this little $50 million business down in New Jersey. One of the students had to go do their internship down there, and I was the advisor. So we went down together to visit the site in the spring before she started in the summer.

◆
§5.p2

This little factory had been run by — it was literally a mom and pop, a husband and wife — and started making plastic sheets. Different colors, different textures, different thicknesses. You needed enough sheet to cover three football fields with this texture, this color and this thickness, they would make it for you. They had all kinds of quality problems.

◆
§5.p3

They had a workforce that would steal each other's tools. They had to lock their toolboxes from each other because they would steal each other's tools. Management would not talk to the hourly workers. The hourly workers wouldn't tell each other anything. They hated each other. It was a unionized shop, and they just couldn't get along. There's a story they told us — there were some people that hated management so much, when they would go to the restroom, they would take their excrement and paint the walls with it. I'm sure they took some plastic gloves or something with them, and leave the carrel with this mess on the walls. And they never could find this person, but they called him the Mad.

◆
§5.p4

So this student who was going to be going down there and spending her summer there, she couldn't believe what she was walking into. So we listened to all this and I said, let's stop at the diner on the way back to Newark airport — you can get a cup of coffee, I'll get some orange juice, let's talk about this. Because I didn't want to talk there at the site.

◆
§5.p5

So we go back, and I said, well, what are you going to do about this environment that you're going into? How are you going to solve their quality problems? She says I don't know. I said, well, I know, and I will fund this. Every Friday you're going to go and buy a box of donuts. You're going to put it at the coffee desk for the hourly workers. You don't necessarily put it up there in front of everyone, but they're going to figure out that you brought them donuts. And then you're going to go out later that morning and you're going to ask them if there's anything you can do, or ask them what the problems are, and if there's anything they think you might be able to do to help things around there.

◆
§5.p6

So you just gave a peace offering of a few donuts, and you're asking for things. I said, you're going to take those things and you're going to take the low-hanging fruit that you can solve. Some of them are going to be impossible things — ignore them, just solve one or two. Make a difference that next week in their environment. And within a week the foreman's going around putting his arm around her, showing her how the machine works, explaining everything to her, telling her, if we had this we could make better quality here. And she would go back and she would translate it for the management, and she would get them to do the investment they needed to do to improve the thing that the foreman had been telling them for six months but got tired of telling them.

◆
§5.p7

There's an important principle here. I don't know anyone who goes home at the end of the day saying, I did a really lousy job today, and I'm proud of it. People go home and they say, I tried to do a good job today but I couldn't do a good job because of these impediments — some manager that won't listen, some manager who yells at them whenever they make a suggestion, some manager who shoots the messenger. If you can get around those things, you'd be surprised at what you can do. They used to call it empowerment. If you actually listen to other people's suggestions, and listen respectfully, and listen with translating ears — they may not be able to articulate it, they didn't go to college necessarily, but if you show them respect, they will show you respect. She was able to have a very successful internship just for a box of donuts every week.

◆
§5.p8

Steve, you've seen the donut effect?

◆
§5.p9

Steve: Yeah, I have. Sure, absolutely it works. I'll add that the UAW also accepts donuts. It's a formidable lubricant for the United Auto Workers as well. I have personal experience with that.

◆
§5.p10

Simple little things. It has nothing to do with technology. This is a structural materials course. Yes, it is a structural materials course, and in those video archive modules there's lots of information, more than you can absorb in one course. But being successful has a lot more to do with donuts and respecting others than all the technology and all the differential equations in the world.

§6. Differential equations, finite element analysis, and Bethlehem Steel [29:05]

§6.p1

If you need a differential equation solved, hire an MIT grad, they can do it. You don't really need to learn how to solve a differential equation. Well, actually you do if you want to graduate from MIT — you've got to learn to solve differential equations, because you won't pass the quizzes. But if every course was like mine, you wouldn't need to solve a differential equation. Don, how many differential equations have you personally solved in your career?

◆
§6.p2

Don: Not a lot. There certainly have been some times where I was working in sort of more of a research environment where that skill was useful, but as a practicing engineer releasing real parts, I don't think you'll ever use a differential equation.

◆
§6.p3

I've only done it two or three times in my career. That's really interesting. I must have spent six months in school learning about differential equations. It's an important skill, and it teaches you a thought process. The methodology you learn by learning about differential equations and how they work is very important. But actually being able to solve a fourth-order differential equation — who cares? There are people that can be hired at a much lower rate than your salary that are happy to do it.

◆
§6.p4

Things like the finite element analysis — I learned finite element analysis when I worked at Bethlehem Steel. They put me in a course. It was the mid-70s, it was sort of a new technology then. I was all excited, I was going to go and solve this spot-welding problem using the finite element analysis. I went to my boss and I said I want to do this project. He says, no, we don't think that's worthwhile. This is in the research department of the second largest steel company in the world. I said, well, I'll do it on my own time because I was interested from an academic point of view. No. I said, well I'll do it on Saturdays. No. I said all I need is an account number to use the mainframe computer — we didn't have personal computers in the 70s. Nope, they wouldn't allow me to do it.

◆
§6.p5

A year later I left Bethlehem Steel, because I realized they were never going to let me do anything until I had paid the price of being there for 30 years and had an attitude lobotomy like all the other managers. They weren't going to allow me to do things. What really irritated me is the exact same problem was solved on the same finite element analysis, and the paper was published in the Welding Journal two years later by a guy from General Electric Research named Art Nied [Nied?], and he won an award for his work. I had the idea, I wanted to do it, I could have had this done, but I had managers who didn't see the value in it.

◆
§6.p6

This is supposed to be research, folks. If I saw the value in it and I knew how to do it I wouldn't need to do it. That was part of the problem. The culture of Bethlehem Steel is they wanted to clone you into their type of person. They didn't want any diversity — and I'm not talking ethnic or racial diversity — they didn't want any diversity of thinking. They wanted everyone to think the same.

§7. Easter eggs, soccer balls, and looking where no one is looking [32:33]

§7.p1

I don't see any Japanese names right now, but I spent a year in Japan. One of the strengths of Japan is they have very fixed ways of doing things. But the Japanese have criticized themselves for not being creative thinkers. Some Japanese are creative thinkers. But real creativity comes from going in another direction than everybody else. When everyone else is heading in one direction, you go in the opposite direction.

◆
§7.p2

Actually, I learned that when I was seven years old at an Easter egg hunt. If you've ever been to a soccer game with eight-year-olds, all the eight-year-olds run towards the ball. They don't realize that if the soccer ball gets kicked away, they all have to run in the opposite direction. The person who could win when they're eight years old with soccer is the one who stays behind and waits for the ball to come out.

◆
§7.p3

Someone said, I found an Easter egg, and everybody goes running over because they figure all the other Easter eggs are right there where they found that one. I remember starting to run over there, and I could see the backs of all these heads, and I realized there were another 20 eyes in those 10 kids looking in the same place. I thought, even as a seven-year-old, I'm going to look over here, because I'll be the only set of eyes looking over here. So I started looking where other people were not looking.

◆
§7.p4

Frankly, that was the source of my professional career. I would look at the field — in my field it was welding — and people were telling me oh, go do this, because the big name in welding, he and his students were looking at welding metallurgy problems. I said no, I'm not going to be another set of eyes looking at the same problems. Those are all bright people, I had respect for their abilities. I wanted to look where no one else was looking. I sometimes liken it to a farmer plowing a field — if you're plowing the same field that has been plowed for a hundred years, it's unlikely that your plow is going to turn over some gemstone. But if you're plowing a brand new field that no one's ever plowed before, it's possible that you might uncover a gemstone.

◆
§7.p5

So it's best to look where other people are not looking, and use a little creativity to ask questions that no one's ever asked before. If you want to know a secret of being an academic — if you ask enough questions that no one's ever asked before, every now and then one of them will work, and you'll become famous for answering a question that no one ever asked before. That's sort of the secret of being an academic, in my opinion. That, and having a lot of money — research money — and being at MIT where you have bright graduate students that you can hire and let them do the thinking, that's even better.

◆
§7.p6

Student: I want to resonate with what the professor said about creativity. When I was at school I was majoring in design, and at that moment there was a big trend on UX/UI — interface, screen design, web design. Most of my classmates went that direction because the industry needs that kind of designer. But the truth is, as humans, we still need the physical stuff. I'm still passionate about object and product design. So when most people do a certain direction, try to do the opposite, because you could show your value and creativity from a different perspective.

◆
§7.p7

That's really what creativity is about. They call things creative because it's something someone hasn't done before. If you're following the crowd — whether it's nanotechnology, additive manufacturing, high-strength ceramics, high-temperature superconductors — everybody's going in the same direction. That's when you need to stop, re-evaluate, do your every-three-year evaluation or your weekly evaluation, and say, do I want to be just part of the crowd, or do I want to go and blaze a new path? Nine times out of ten that new path may be fruitless, but every now and then you find a new path that's fruitful. And then people say, oh, that's the such-and-such effect, and they use your last name to describe it.

◆
§7.p8

Steve: Just to echo Professor Eagar's point. In the mid-2000s working at Mercedes in Germany, they were terrified of Lexus. They used to sit around and talk about how scared they were of Lexus eating their lunch. They kept focusing on how they were going to beat Lexus. At the same time Elon Musk was going a totally different direction in electric cars. Guess who ended up eating Mercedes' lunch? Tesla. Talk about the success of going a different direction.

§7.p9

There's a great example for you. There's lots of examples if you look for them. So this has become more of a — not a structural materials recitation, which is fine. I'm more interested in giving you some thoughts from our experience. If you're willing to listen to a bunch of old people talk about their experience — that actually is a little bit of respect. Do you have some respect for people who have already made the mistakes that you're going to make? Understanding that if you go out there into a factory, you don't have to solve the technical problems. The first thing you have to solve are the people problems, the communication problems.
