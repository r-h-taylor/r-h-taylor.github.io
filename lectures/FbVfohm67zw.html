§3. Science versus engineering [09:48]

§3.p1

Anybody have any questions about anything? So my summary is, science seeks to increase human knowledge, and engineering seeks to improve the human condition. If you go back with various definitions we've had of science and engineering, you'll find the engineer somehow — when the first day I asked you what's engineering, one of you said something about benefiting society. When you talk to a scientist, they like to talk about increase in knowledge.

◆
§3.p2

And they say — I've been listening to this all my professional career — that we need to do more science, because doing more science will bring us great new things like the transistor. Well, it turns out the guys who won the Nobel Prize, Bardeen, Shockley, and Brattain at Bell Labs, developed the transistor. But they were doing it because they wanted a highly reliable switch. Everybody says, oh, this came out of basic research. No. It came out of the fact that in the old days — you see it on the old movies, you see the switchboard and operators making the connections. Then they got to the point where they had switches, and just down the street from me is a great big AT&T building — it just be full of switches in the old days. ◆
§3.p3

So they were looking at the reliability of the whole network just going to fall apart, as they extrapolated into the future. They needed a more reliable switch, and so they were actually trying to find an electronic switch. That wasn't basic science, that was applying basic physics to an actual need. So you always hear, jump-starting basic science was what brought us the transistor. No, it was a specific need of society to have a bigger communication system that wasn't going to be constantly breaking down. And electronic switches were known to be about a million times more reliable than the mechanical switches.

§4. The hierarchy of snobbery: scientist, engineer, doctor [18:46]

§4.p1

So I consider the hierarchy of snobbery, that I call it. If you talk about scientists, engineers, and medical clinicians — a scientist, the goal is to increase human knowledge; an engineer, to improve the human condition; and a medical doctor, to improve human health. The scientist just wants to do something to discover something new. They don't really care if it has an application. Take the definitions, listen to what the scientists say. Are they saying I need to do this for the application it's going to solve? If they're saying this is to improve the human condition, they're probably doing engineering, or medicine. They're not doing real science. The basic scientists, the people studying the big bang theory, the people trying to understand what's going on in outer space — it's not clear that that's going to really help the people who are starving in Bangladesh.

◆
§4.p2

The engineer is trying to do good, the medical doctor is trying to do no harm. That's their mantra, but in fact, they're trying to do good. The old Hippocratic oath is do no harm. The scientists will study anything because anything is good — everything's fair game for the scientists for studying. The engineer, if you remember von Karman, create something that never was. So the engineer's trying to create something, and that thing is probably going to be something that's useful. In fact, engineers look down on people that are doing things — just like I'm looking down right now on the scientists who are doing useless things — because to the engineer, the ultimate thing is to do something useful. Same thing for the medical clinician, they're going to administer to the sick, and also nowadays the healthy to keep them from getting sick.

◆
§4.p3

But the service for the engineer and the medical doctor is to society. It's not clear who the service is to for the scientist. They just want society to provide them with the resources for them to go out in their sandbox and do what they want to do. You may be saying it pretty strongly, but that's what I've been listening to for many years, and that's the argument. So when you hear these arguments, you can tell if the person is really a scientist or if they're really an engineer or a medical person, depending on who they're trying to help.

§5.p2

§5.p3

§6. Cultural differences in hierarchy — Asia and the West [27:21]

§6.p1

And as he explained this to me — as I kept on saying, what do you mean what do I want — because in my culture it's actually almost a crime for the professor to extract something from the person beneath them that they have supervisory authority over. §6.p2

Well, they are as creative, but they've been trained to think in the Confucianism style of certain rules and doing things by the norm and being obedient. §6.p3

§7. The German General Staff and the manager's promotion ladder [30:00]

§7.p1

Nibley went on to talk about the German High Command, the General Staff, which after World War Two — tried desperately for a hundred years, going back to the 1880s, to train up a generation of leaders for the German army, but it never worked, because the men who delighted their superiors, the managers, got the high commands, while the men who delighted the lower ranks, the leaders, got reprimands. And as I started to rise up in 1990 through the MIT administration, I saw this all the time. I could spot the person within minutes of whether they were a manager or a leader. Were they trying to suck up to the people who were the bosses above them, or were they trying to help the people that they were put in charge of?

◆
§7.p2

I see it among faculty supervisors of students. Are they telling the students what to do and expecting obedience, and are they thinking for the students? Or are they someone that says, okay, here's the laboratory, it's your sandbox, I want you to solve this problem, and letting them have the freedom to do it? Where do you think you get more creativity? The latter, because two heads are better than one. And in fact, the advisor — faculty advisor-student mentorship, if it is a partnership, it is two heads, or five heads if you're part of a group, thinking together.

◆
§7.p3

There's a faculty member who's passed away now in this department, he would sit down every week with his graduate students and he would go over their lab notebook every week. Took a lot of time, and the graduate students sort of feared going in to see him, because he would start nitpicking at little things. He was actually from Minnesota, and he was a wonderful man in many ways, but he had learned this sort of management way that it was his job to hand the knowledge down to the student. This particular professor — I won't rub his name into the ground, because he was a very kind person in many ways — but he just thought his job was to tell people how to do things because somehow he had more knowledge than anybody else.

◆
§9.p2

"Do not promote individuals whose competence might threaten their own position, and so as the power of management spreads ever wider, the quality deteriorates, if that's possible.
