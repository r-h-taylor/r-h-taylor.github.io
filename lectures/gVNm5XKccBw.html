§2. Externalities: pollution, embargo, war, culture [07:27]

§2.p1

We started talking about externalities. When I taught this course a few years ago I used to say the most important parameters were price and availability of a material. Well it turns out more and more those are taking a back seat to the externalities. Does anyone remember the definition of externality? An externality in economics is something where someone else has to bear the price of something they had not intended to bear the price of. That could be air pollution, water pollution — it could be political, economic, social, environmental, military. Lots of different things are now influencing material selection more than just the basic properties of materials.

§2.p2

Not because they had a monopoly on the availability of the rare earth metal ores, but because they had been willing to accept the pollution and to pay their workers very low wages, because that's the going rate in China. They had a de facto monopoly with a hurdle of probably five years before someone else could enter the market. You can't just stop all the production of electronics in Japan for five years — you're going to have to pay the price somewhere. Same type of thing as the oil embargo of '73.

§3. Regulated monopolies and the transistor [19:19]

§3.p1

Regulatory. Utilities and the rate base. Anybody know what a rate base is for a utility? A regulated monopoly. We don't have two companies that deliver electricity to your home, we have one. In the old days we had something called American Telephone and Telegraph — we actually still do, but it was a regulated monopoly in the old days. In a regulated monopoly the regulators in the government will actually set the profitability of the company. People used to think, wasn't it foresightful for AT&T to run Bell Laboratories and spend all this money on basic research. Well that's a load of bull. AT&T had a promise from the regulators that they could make six percent profit on whatever they could justify spending, and they could justify spending on a huge research facility. They did a lot of great basic research, but almost all of it was aimed at improving the productivity and the reliability of the telephone system.

◆
§3.p2

You'll hear, oh they invented the transistor, that came out of basic research. Another fallacy. The transistor was developed because in 1925 AT&T had statisticians who were looking at the reliability of the phone system at the time. It was little mechanical switches — you see the old TVs and the telephone operator taking the plug and putting it from one hole to another, that was how they were switching. But when they were putting the plug in, it was a mechanical plug, and anyone who looks at mechanical things knows that after ten thousand hours of operation or whatever, they tend to fatigue or wear and they break. So from 1925 on, Bell Laboratories was trying to find a replacement for the mechanical switch. They could plot the increased use of phone usage, and they knew that sometime in the 1950s the entire system would break down — because to make a phone call from A to B you would have to go through so many switches that the probability of failure would be horrendous, and the system would just collapse because it would be unreliable. The transistor came out of an applied study to find an electrical switch that would be more reliable than a mechanical switch. How many of you have heard that the transistor is evidence of how basic research pays off? Well it's not basic research, it was applied research. It was a regulated industry — they could make six percent for every dollar they could spend, they just had to justify that what they were spending it on had some basis in improving the phone system.

◆
§3.p3

That's still true in the utilities business — the electrical utilities, the water utilities. They have commissions usually run by the state — in Massachusetts it's the Department of Public Utilities — and they basically set the profitability of these companies. They look at them critically and they'll say, we'll let you make five percent so that people who invest in your stocks can put them through pension funds, you make enough money to retire, and whatever you can afford to spend you can spend. Anyone gone to the store and bought some LED light bulbs that were discounted because the local utility was paying seven dollars for that light bulb? Why do they do that?

Student: [answer about energy savings]

That's part of it, but it's really an economic reason.

§3.p4

To build a new power plant — a big one comes in at a thousand megawatts of electric power generation, a thousand megawatts is a billion watts — the installed price to build that plant is about two thousand dollars a kilowatt. If you could save electricity at less than two thousand dollars a kilowatt, it's more efficient for society to give you efficient light bulbs. If they can give you a discount on the cost of a light bulb for less than two thousand dollars a kilowatt over the life of that light bulb, it's a win-win. The regulators basically say, we'll let you make a little more on all these discounts you'll give on energy-efficient equipment and light bulbs, so you won't have to tear out half of Belmont to build a generating station. Having lived in Belmont — no one wants the coal-fired plant in their backyard, not even a gas-fired, quiet plant. They don't even want hydroelectric plants. They really don't want any electric plant in their backyard.

§3.p5

Take the people on Martha's Vineyard, Nantucket. They don't want those windmills destroying their view of the ocean. Now wait a second. If you told them we're going to triple your electric bill because that's what you want — would they say yes or would they say no? But that's not what people tell them; they say oh, it's going to destroy my view. What is the choice? We're now paying over twenty cents a kilowatt-hour in Massachusetts. Those are externalities on what we choose to do.

§4. Embargoes, transportation, and the Saugus ironworks [25:46]

§4.p1

Embargoes. There were blood diamonds in Angola back longer ago than — actually, when your parents were in diapers. Rhodesia, which is now part of Zimbabwe, just northeast of South Africa — they had the world's best chromium ore reserves. They had a civil war going on, and the world tried to put pressure on them, just like we try to put pressure on North Korea and Iran and other countries to not build nuclear weapons. Rhodesia wasn't building nuclear weapons, but it was a lot of pain and suffering for a lot of people there. So we basically said we're going to put an embargo on Rhodesian chrome. They had the best chrome ore in the world — it was so good you could basically just take that rock and crush it and throw it into your steel furnace to make stainless steel. That's how good it was. No one else had chromium that good. We used to have iron ore that good — it was called the Mesabi Range in Minnesota, and it ran out about 1948, but it was so good we could crush the rock and put it right into the blast furnace to make steel. We didn't have to process it. We had the world's cheapest iron ore in the United States, but we used it all up.

◆
§4.p2

Rhodesia had the best chrome, and you couldn't fake Rhodesian chrome. There was nowhere else in the world that had anywhere near that quality. But we weren't supposed to be importing it, and we couldn't keep them from exporting it. It turns out they still sold it on the black market, and the people using it knew it was Rhodesian chrome, but it got laundered through some other country, and the customs agency kind of looked the other way, even though we knew we were still supporting the civil war in Rhodesia. Officially we weren't. So there are embargoes that work and don't work. Same thing in the oil business — the Nigerians are always cheating on the Saudi Arabians on the oil cartel.

◆
§4.p3

Transportation as an externality. My example here is copper tubing. We think copper tubing is expensive, but it's not that expensive. All through the 1980s and 1990s, when all these metal-producing industries were going offshore to lower labor rates, we never lost the six factories in the United States that made copper tubing. Reading, Pennsylvania had this old plant — I had a student do an LGO thesis down there at Reading Tube — and there were six of these plants in the United States. The reason was, when you ship copper tubing you're shipping a lot of air in the hole — large volume. It's expensive but it's not that expensive — go to a hardware store and you might have to pay eighty bucks for a ten-foot length. The shipping costs were too great, and so we actually kept those industries because of the transportation costs. There are a few other industries like that, but that's one example.

◆
§4.p4

My other example is Saugus Ironworks. Have you been to Saugus Ironworks, in Saugus, Massachusetts, just up the north shore here? Saugus Ironworks was actually another transportation story, but one where it was an energy crisis in the early seventeenth century. Saugus Ironworks was 1619 or so. In the sixteenth and seventeenth century in England they were having an energy crisis. What was the source of energy in England then? Wood. They were deforesting England, and they had three uses of trees — we'll talk about this later. One was to make cast iron for military purposes, to make cannons for your ships. Another was for shipbuilding — you needed the great big masts for the sailing ships. And another — it turns out they were starting to make window glass. I told you the story about bullseye glass, and we'll talk about that some more. They had to use charcoal to make the iron and to make the glass, and then you needed great big trees for the masts of the big sailing ships, man-o'-wars and so on.

◆
§4.p5

England was being deforested and they were having an energy crisis, and they were passing laws — I'll read you some of the historical story behind all that. They essentially built an ironworks when they learned about the new world. They sent people over here. They built a glassworks down in Jamestown, Virginia. They came over and built the glassworks in Jamestown Virginia — why? Because they needed trees, and if there was anything they had on the east coast of North America at that time, we had trees. Up here they built an ironworks, Saugus Ironworks, a national historic site now, and we'll talk about that more later. It wasn't a transfer — this was a case where it was worth it because you had such high energy costs in England, to come over here to get your energy, which was trees. So the energy crisis of 1973 was not the first energy crisis.

§7. The Future of Metals: why steel still wins [43:09]
