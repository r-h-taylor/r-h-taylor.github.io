§2. Steel productivity and the 1980s crisis [04:52]

§2.p1

We finished up talking about productivity of steel. Even in my lifetime there's been a change from about six person-hours per ton to about a third of a person-hour per ton. That's a factor of about 20 in productivity. As a result of that change in productivity, all through the 1980s — about 1980 it was 6 person-hours a ton, by the end of the '80s about 3 person-hours per ton — because they had already put in continuous casting and basic oxygen furnaces, and the labor rates went down because of those things, all of a sudden there was a worldwide overcapacity, and the steel companies all started losing money.

◆
§2.p2

There was only one steel company I know of in the United States that never had an unprofitable quarter all through the 1980s. It was Allegheny Steel. Anybody know why they never had an unprofitable quarter? In my opinion it's because they had a CEO, a guy named Richard Simmons — not the guy who does the exercises, but Dick Simmons of Simmons Hall. §2.p3

§7. Bonding potential and the limits of materials [41:00]

§7.p1

What I really want to point out is there are limits to materials, and superconductors are an excellent example. You have to know not just one property. This is fracture toughness and density. If you look at these Ashby plots there's a limit. Things only get so light. Polymer foams or cork or bolts of wood are very light, but they don't have very much strength. This is toughness and strength — you'd love to be super light and super strong. You'd love to be over here, super light and super strong. But there's nothing that exists over there.

◆
§7.p2

Fundamentally it's because everything we know about materials is related to their bonding. Dr. Belmar put up a plot like this, which is the energy versus distance between two atoms. If I bring two atoms together in space, just two atoms alone, they will find an equilibrium distance of separation, which we often call a₀, because this is the energy potential well. It's an electrostatic energy that goes as 1 over R² — you learned about electrostatic energy in freshman physics. This is a nuclear repulsive term, and it goes as something like R to the 8th or R to the 10th, and the two compete with each other. If you try to squeeze them any tighter, they just don't want to squeeze together. He pointed out that this curve is actually steeper than that curve. If you blow up this region right here, it's actually asymmetric, not a nice symmetric parabola. He talked about how you can explain Poisson ratio because of the energy potential. In the old days, the first ones in the 1920s that the quantum-mechanics people generated were called the Lennard-Jones potential. They've got all kinds of names nowadays.

§7.p3

The curvature in here is nothing more than the modulus. So if we're talking about modulus of elasticity — let me do a force plot. The force is equal to dE/dR. The force plot looks something like this, where a₀ is where it crosses the zero force. That's the equilibrium distance of separation. If I look at this thing coming down here, that's a zero, and so you've got a force. And if you differentiate the force you get the modulus, which is going to be proportional to ∂²E/∂R². There's a math, to prove I'm an MIT professor.

§7.p4

Most of what we know about strength of materials is contained right here, in the bonding potential between those atoms. That's for structural materials. And everything you need to know — magnetic properties, optical properties, piezoelectric properties — is contained, if you know everything about where the electrons are and how free they are in the material. So I'm not teaching a course on structural materials or on functional materials — in structural materials you need to know the Lennard-Jones bonding potential, and from this fundamentally you could generate everything else in the world. That's what computational material science today is all about.

§7.p5

They were able to do this for very simple systems in 1925 out of quantum mechanics, but they didn't have the computing horsepower to do it for more complex systems until somewhere in the 1990s, when the computers got powerful enough that they could start doing computational material science. Now a third of the faculty in this department are working on computational material science. Why? Well, it's a new tool, the computer, to be able to do this.
