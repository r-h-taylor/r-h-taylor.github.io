§1. Inspection and the weld-angle criteria [00:02]

§1.p1

§1.p3

§1.p5

§2. The rubber-sealed pressure vessel and leak-before-break [11:30]

§2.p1

Another example of things that are not accounted for in the design: a pressure vessel with a factor of safety of four. On the inside, they had a rubber seal in the bottom — the rubber was at the bottom, working its way up a certain amount. We don't know the intent from the designer, but one advantage of doing this is you can reduce the exposure to oxygen. You can reduce the temperature of exposure for the metal. You can hide a welded connection that may not be as corrosion resistant as the cylinder itself. And it's the region where you have the most hydrostatic pressure, just from gravity.

◆
§2.p2

They had an overpressure, and they found at the very end that the crack was unzipping around the circumference — which in principle is the direction where you only have half the stress. What happened is the crack somehow knew that it was being sealed. The crack grew all around with the rubber bridging it, so it didn't leak.

◆
§2.p3

That's a concept I remember when I was a student — the professor said, "leak before break." So I said fine, how exactly do you do that? Because I was not exposed to it. Everybody knows what leak before break means? You want to be able to sustain a crack that grows through the thickness, and allow for a leak before you reach the fracture toughness where the crack just unravels. So you allow for not critical crack growth — subcritical. It's growing by fatigue or creep to a point where you go through the thickness and you start leaking, and people know there's a problem, and they have to come and fix it. As opposed to growing a crack up to ninety percent — and I've seen it.

◆
§2.p4

So say we have a vessel and we're looking at it from the top down. From the inside, because of some chemicals, there's a crack that grew like this — a semi-elliptical crack that creates this stress intensity at the front of the crack. What you want is, given your thickness, you require the material to be tough enough so the crack can actually grow all the way through. Leak, and people react to it and fix it. So the main requirement is not based on the fatigue resistance. It's only based on the fracture toughness of the material. That's the K value, related to the stress times the square root of pi A. A is the length of the crack. And then we can add a little fudge factor in front. So the idea is, for a given application, you want A to reach T, given your level of stress, and all of this to be inferior to the toughness of the material.

◆
§2.p5

Now when you come up with all of this, you're assuming that the crack is not going to seal itself. And that wasn't the case with this vessel, because of that rubber membrane. We have projects where we see copper pipes that have holes that go all the way through the thickness by pitting corrosion, and they seal themselves. If it's hot water, you have a little bit of flow. All the salts, and maybe some organics from the water, just come through the crack and seal it. So what are you going to do about this? Are you going to also be inspecting for stain? Because most of the time it is visible, but as a stain — a white stain, as opposed to a leak.

◆
§2.p6

In this example, that's the idea: if you're looking carefully at the bottom of this pressure vessel, you'll see the rubber. You were probably able to see the rubber before it cracked. But was that type of structure subject to an in-service inspection? Most of the time it's not. There's no necessarily visual inspection schedule. It depends on the type of factory, but in some factories they wouldn't have anything very specific to look for — little bulges of rubber, or stain coming from a leak that's opening and sealing. So it comes back to this idea that you don't design with a factor of safety that's minimal, thinking, "it can break, it'll be fine." You have redundancy in your safety. You don't want it to get into this leaking situation. If it happens, you hope that inspection's going to catch it, or that it will actually leak and relieve the pressure.

◆
§2.p7

There's nothing perfect. It becomes a question of acceptable risk. The reason they were able to do this for a while is they thought, "it's going to improve our design." When they started getting feedback like this, I'm sure they had to step back and say, "we'll let it corrode and leak. It's just safer." That's where the history comes in. So I think you have the idea as far as design and manufacturing — how you can affect your factor of safety in different ways.

§3. Statistics, demand and capacity [18:46]

§3.p1

So we're going to go ahead with the statistics for today. Statistics typically work backward. If you try to come up with a new design and you want to do a statistical design, I'm sure you can — is that going to be very helpful to you? Probably not. It would only be helpful if you look back to a similar application and find out how they consumed the factor of safety by experience — how they designed it very precisely, what's their failure rate, and what is it attributed to. As opposed to just saying, "I'm going to assume that the material properties are going to be plus or minus twenty percent of typical value." Where did that come from? You need to have a basis for what you're putting in.

◆
§3.p2

That's why it's easier to do it after the fact, and that's why it's very hard to start from scratch with a very optimized design. When Honda started to do these little ATVs, they were clearly overdesigned. They were much stronger and much more long-lasting than they probably wanted them to be, but they wanted to penetrate the market, and they didn't have the history of all of this being used everywhere, and they were somehow able to afford it. The exhaust system, everything — things would just last forever. And they changed. They went back and started to optimize based on history. So I don't want to create the false idea that you'll start from scratch, take some notes, go to some handbooks and come up with a statistical design for your new product. You can do it in an existing product. And that's more what we're going to be doing for the next twenty minutes or so.

◆
§3.p3

This is just a normal distribution for failure where things will fail after a mean amount of time, with things that will fail prematurely for various reasons or at a lower load, and things that will last longer. It's very easy when you think about a simple parameter. So here we're looking at it in 2D. One standard deviation, two standard deviations — if you go away by one standard deviation, sixty-something percent of your failures are going to be within that range, plus or minus one standard deviation.

◆
§3.p4

This is the reference I'm using for the statistical discussion. It's not a book that you're going to design something new with, but it's helpful to understand why people do the things the way they do. We talked about demand and capacity in the last class, and here we have it in an X and Y plot. There's some uncertainty on each. That's why our overall design — or manufactured structure or product — is going to behave in a certain way where most of the time it's going to be away from that failure boundary at 45 degrees.

◆
§3.p5

But as you change your conditions, what it's being exposed to outside the precise value that you've put on your design — that's where your safety factor goes down, and you get close to that failure boundary. In this case they overlap, here on the last circle. There'll be a given combination of load and strength that will result in failure. The probability of failure is constant around that circle. That's just assuming that each parameter is working on the positive side and the negative side in the same way. We have a circle because we're assuming that we have a twenty percent variation in capacity and a twenty percent variation in demand. It's not the general case.

◆
§3.p6

If we look at this, one thing to keep in mind is, in a worst-case scenario, if I go pretty bad just on the design, I'll still be okay on the capacity side. If I'm changing the load applied, same thing. But when there's something that was not what I intended as a nominal value both ways, that's where we have the failure here at an angle in the chart.

◆
§3.p7

And it doesn't have to be straight. That's why now we look at it in 3D. It's still the same thing, where we have the demand and the capacity, and this cut is at an angle here for the failure. This is more or less saying — and I think it's probably fair — that you can break just about anything. Everything that's being built and is being sold can be broken, one way or another. Sometimes because that specific one was not exactly as strong as the others. Sometimes because you just put too much load on it. So that concept is almost universal. And what we're going to go through is see how, after the fact, we can determine this probability of failure.

§4. Time, social risk, and the bridge case [25:33]

§4.p1

The criterion for being in that zone — this looks big, but it depends. It depends on what period of time you're looking at. If you look at it when you get a product right off the factory, this looks big. But if you look at it at a probability of failure over 10 years — maybe for an aircraft 20 years — every day there's a different load, every month or year they do significant repairs on your structure. The cut in the pie would probably start very close to zero when it's coming out of the factory, and then as time goes, it's just going to creep up on you. That's taken into account in this basic criterion, where you have a multiple factor. So you have time coming in, or frequency coming in. And then you have, for various structures, this social criterion factor.

◆
§4.p2

What it says is, if you're looking at something that a lot of people would be exposed to a failure of, like a dam, you want to minimize even further your risk. If you have 2,000 people on a bridge, it's not like my tire blowing up because it's a hot day and I was in park at the right location. Now these factors — when we're looking down here for towers, masts, and offshore structures — I think the offshore structure is really true. When Hurricane Cino [?] or some of the Gulf storms come in, it's not atypical to have a whole platform just collapsing. You hope that before they do, they're able to shut the well. But the basic idea is that it's out there — there are very few people exposed to it. It's worth a lot of money, but it becomes more of an economical decision rather than a people-safety decision.

◆
§4.p3

When we're looking at the Longfellow Bridge, just across here — it's more than a hundred years old. If there was a risk for it to really fail, we'd have to shut the Red Line and get it fixed, because there are a lot of people that count on it every single day. Just the train going on is different than a few cars and bicycles every once in a while. It's creating the highest loads and exposing a lot of people at the same time.

◆
§4.p4

So that's how, in the book, they go about figuring out why people do things a certain way. If we're looking at a bridge — I thought, when I looked at this, that the factor for a bridge was not that high. ◆
§4.p5

So here we are with the question of time, because this shows a different probability. As time goes by, these little probabilities are going to become more and more important. I specifically picked the factor of two as the initial safety factor. This is what they call a static safety factor. So the real factor of safety comes down to about 1.2. That's taking into account some maintenance, some inspections, some variation in loads that were not expected. In the case of a bridge — bigger cars, smaller cars, more salt on the road, more lanes — you don't really know what's coming up with a bridge.

◆
§4.p6

That's why you put a safety factor to begin with. But that safety factor becomes significant when we have this distribution — the normal probability distribution. I'm looking at it for 5 years first. Say this is three times the standard deviation, where we put the six sigma. You've heard of six sigma?

◆
§4.p7

This is something I could probably test for, if I really wanted to, with my design. This is something I would very hardly test for in practice. The way I get there is by inferring that I've done my job up to a certain interval that I was able to. And that's where probability and risk and your factor of safety are changing. When you change between something you can measure — you can predict this by testing, or maybe you just do one sigma, but a certain value here you can test — there's a certain point where really it's not practical, and the only way you can do it is by looking at the history. If I start with a factor of safety of two based on what I know, I end up with 1.2.
