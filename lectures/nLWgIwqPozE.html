§3. Aluminum design, fatigue, and the welding code [07:48]

§3.p1

If you look at the old aluminum structural welding code, before 2000 it was thicker than the one today, but it had a design section. By 2003 it actually got thinner, because they included the Aluminum Association's aluminum design manual, which has a fair amount of thickness to it. If you look at allowable design stresses for aluminum, you'll find — for all kinds of structures — they have fatigue design details in greater concentration than you'll find in the steel literature. Does anybody know anything about the properties of aluminum that make fatigue a greater problem for aluminum than for steel? No one?

◆
§3.p2

If you look at stress versus number of cycles for a steel, you get a fatigue curve that comes down and looks like this. High stress and low numbers of cycles, log cycles. Somewhere around a million or 10 million cycles the thing levels off for steel, and has essentially infinite life, which they usually draw as an arrow going out. For aluminum, you'll be considerably weaker, but the curve just keeps going down. People have gone out to 10 to the 8th or 10 to the 9th cycles — it takes a long time to get to 10 to the 9th cycles — but it just keeps decreasing. For steel the fatigue limit is somewhere between 0.45 and 0.5 of the yield stress. For aluminum, there is no fatigue limit. If you fatigue the aluminum long enough, the acceptable stress level will keep going down. This is a big problem for introducing aluminum wheels in trucks.

◆
§3.p3

They had aluminum wheels in cars back in the sixties and seventies, but it wasn't until the nineties that aluminum wheels in trucks took off, and that was when they got sophisticated enough computers to do a good enough stress design to know exactly what the stress was. They were using supercomputers to design truck wheels out of aluminum. Aluminum has this problem of having no fatigue limit below which the material will not fail in fatigue.

◆
§3.p4

The aluminum design manual has all kinds of connections, bolted or welded, in different categories A through F if I remember. The E categories are not quite as bad as F, but things where you wrap a weld around and you have lots of residual stresses, you end up getting fatigue cracks fairly easily. There are some famous bridge failures that occurred because of that type of doubler-plate design where they wrapped around. There's one in Australia that was a big surprise about fifty or sixty years ago to the designers of steel. A lot of those details have come exactly out of the steel construction manual in terms of weld-type details, but they apply to aluminum in a lot more complex ways.

◆
§3.p5

Aluminum doesn't have the strength of steel. It's about one-third the strength of steel. You can get 60, 70 ksi yield aluminum alloys, heat-treated aerospace alloys, but they're not very weldable. Most of the weldable aluminum alloys that can develop full strength in the weld — there's a host of alloys with a number of different filler metals. You can't just use any old filler metal with aluminum. If you use the wrong filler metal with the wrong alloy, it's going to crack, and you can't prevent it. There are some aluminum alloys that just cannot be welded without cracking. A lot of the 2000-series alloys that they rivet aircraft out of — they rivet them because they can't weld them. If you try to do fusion welding on a 2024 aluminum, you just have cracks in the heat-affected zone.

◆
§3.p6

What they typically use for welding of ships are 5000-series alloys. The problem with those is they've only got 20 or 30 ksi yield strength. That's significantly less than something like an HY-100, which is what you build a submarine out of today, at 100 ksi. So it's one quarter the strength of submarine steel, about half the strength of plate on a surface ship. So aluminum doesn't have the yield strength, it doesn't have the fatigue strength, and you have to be very careful about what filler metal you use.

◆
§3.p7

You can hear about wonderful aluminum alloys with tremendous strength. Anybody have an idea what the highest-technology aluminum alloys are used for — alloys close to 100 ksi yield?

◆
§3.p8

Student: Baseball bats.

◆
§3.p9

Baseball bats. They slim them down, and they have special alloys used only for baseball bats. They heat them up and they quench them in ice water in order to maintain fine grain size, and they will get higher strengths than anything used in any aircraft. But they're very thin — about twenty thousandths thick in some spots.

◆
§3.p10

So it's sometimes interesting to find out where the real technology is. If you look at the market size, it makes sense. Some of the most sophisticated stainless steel goes into razor blades. Why? There are a lot of razor blades out there. There's this complex table that you don't really have to understand right now, but you should know that if you're welding a particular base metal, there are all kinds of weld metals, and you can only choose certain combinations, and it's much more restrictive than steel.

◆
§3.p11

The structural welding code for steel is actually fairly simple. It basically says the safety factor for a building is 1.67 and for a bridge it's 2.0. But for aluminum, the design stress level becomes a function of the alloy you're using. The strengths you can use — remember I told you 20 to 30 ksi yield strength — the fatigue strengths for allowable shear are significantly less. You can only use about one-third of your yield strength. So you only have one half to one quarter of the yield strength to begin with compared to steel. But instead of being able to use about sixty percent of that, which you can in steel, you can only use one-third. So you're getting down to design stresses, in some cases — well, the 1100 series, like frying pans, is relatively pure aluminum, or aluminum foil — but you're getting down to design stresses in the five-to-eight ksi range, which is not much stress at all. It's not much more than the yield strength of a good plastic. So it might be aluminum, but in terms of fatigue strength long-term, it's really not much better than plastic.

◆
§3.p12

It's got more toughness than plastic, and it has certain other advantages. It will burn but it's harder to burn. You end up with a very complex manual, much more complex than a steel construction manual, just because the aluminum metallurgy is much more difficult than the steel metallurgy. We've got a proliferation where we've gone from a manual fifteen years ago where everything you needed to know was in one volume, to now a manual of nearly that size plus all of this. You really have to be a fairly sophisticated civil or structural engineer to do it. I used to know how to do it in the 1997 code, but now I go get an expert on aluminum design. It's just a lot messier.

◆
§3.p13

That's part of the explosion of the codes I've talked about. The codes have gotten much more complex, which means you need more and more specialty. It's the same in most things, whether it's medicine or ship design. At the same time, I'm finding more and more entrepreneurs, small businesses that start up and decide we don't need all this engineering overhead, we can go market our product, and we'll just let our suppliers do our engineering. You end up with new products, some of which are successful, but some of which become a disaster.

§4. Corrugated stainless steel tubing: a new product enters the market [18:36]

§4.p1

[Tom holds up a length of corrugated stainless steel tubing.] What I wanted to talk about is corrugated stainless steel tubing. Anyone ever heard of corrugated stainless steel tubing? This is gas tubing. It's fuel gas, originally in yellow, because that's the international code for fuel gas tubing. A plumber goes in a building, or a firefighter goes into a building, and they see yellow tubing — they know it probably carries a flammable gas, sort of a warning.

◆
§4.p2

It replaced black iron pipe. In the early eighties the American Gas Association or the Gas Research Institute — I can't remember which, they're both located in Ohio if I remember — decided they wanted to promote more use of gas. You can understand why. They were also concerned that most of the steel piping they'd been putting in in the twenties and thirties and forties and fifties was corroding. They buried the steel pipe in the ground, and particularly in New England — in Arizona it's not so bad because it doesn't rain that often, but in New England it rains a lot, the ground is wet, and this stuff corrodes. It's about an eighth of an inch thick in small diameters, but it takes a while to corrode. The U.S. Army did a study of 25,000 underground storage tanks, and they showed the typical corrosion rate: you'll perforate an eighth of an inch of steel in 5 to 25 years, depending on soil conditions, part of the country, amount of rainfall, pH of the soil, electrical resistivity of the soil because of the corrosion currents that can be carried through. Typical time to perforation of a pipe is 5 to 25 years.

◆
§4.p3

§5. CSST: Japanese origin, ANSI LC1, and market entry [27:11]

§5.p1

So they had concerns about all this corroding gas piping in the early eighties, and they were looking for solutions. What's the piping they put in now? It's plastic. The plastic won't corrode. The plastic has other problems — it's brittle, it probably won't last an earthquake, but if we have that bad an earthquake a lot of other things won't last either. In Japan they had developed corrugated stainless steel tubing for gas, because they have a lot of earthquakes and they were worried about fires. If I had a longer length of this, you'd find it's very flexible, comes in big coils three or four feet in diameter. Costs about five dollars a foot.

◆
§5.p2

Between 1987 and 1997, to market this in the U.S., they hire a guy out in Waltham, Massachusetts who works for Foster Wheeler. He writes a standard called ANSI LC1. They incorporate that standard in Canada, because the Canadian authorities to develop a new standard for gas piping have less bureaucracy than the United States. Since they get it approved in Canada, the Canadian standard essentially gets reciprocity as a U.S. standard, and it becomes an ANSI standard. They will now sell it to you — all of about sixty pages long, it'll cost you $600. I just bought a copy for $598. Again, tells you about the price of these things.

◆
§5.p3

They incorporate the standard on how to install this stuff, because you have to have special fittings. There are about half a dozen manufacturers of it now. By 1997 they start marketing it because they've gotten approval from various state agencies to allow this into the plumbing code. Before that, the code wouldn't allow you to use an innovative material. There's how codes stifle innovation. Here's this wonderful new material that the Japanese have been using, and we couldn't use it until they spent ten years getting enough states to approve it that certain companies would start manufacturing it.

◆
§5.p4

In fact, we've been using this stuff for fifty years. You can go to Home Depot and look up how to install a gas stove or a gas dryer, and they will sell you links from three feet to six feet long of corrugated connectors, made by two other people, and they've been making them for fifty years, so you can pull your appliance out from the wall. My furnace at home is a gas furnace, and it's hard-piped with black iron pipe. I can't pull it out from the wall. But your stove, you want to be able to get behind your stove to clean all those little rat droppings. Or you want to pull out your dryer to clean all the lint from behind there.

◆
§5.p5

So we had been using this stuff, but we hadn't been using it in 100 to 200 foot lengths in homes. Why is it such a wonderful product? It's light, it's flexible. Instead of having to thread fifteen or twenty pieces to go from the gas meter to your stove, you have one piece and you can thread it through the walls without cutting down the walls. It's just easy to install. Black iron pipe might cost 70 cents a foot, this stuff is five dollars a foot — it has less value of material in it than black iron pipe, even though it's stainless steel — but they get a tremendous markup. Between '97 and today, fifteen years, there's a billion feet of this stuff out there in American homes.

◆
§5.p6

Anyone have a relative — most of you are probably too young to have big homes with fireplaces on the second floor in the master bedroom, or a gas fireplace in the den — do your parents have homes like that? Yeah, okay. And they'll have gas piped out to your barbecue grill in some places, rather than using a propane tank. Getting some nods. Why? This is the miracle product that makes it possible. We used to have maybe fifty feet of black iron pipe in a home, we now might have 200 feet of this stuff, and we have gas appliances all over the place. Fireplaces in the den and the master bedroom, barbecue grills — everything's becoming gas. So it served the function the Gas Association wanted, to promote the use of gas.

§6. Lightning: the unconsidered failure mode [32:37]

§6.p1

They started selling in 1997. By 1998 they started noticing they had a few more fires than they were used to that were not explained. This is an innovative product, but no one ever thought about what would be the effect of lightning on this product. The effect of lightning is that if lightning hits, it will blow a hole in it. [Tom indicates perforated samples.] Now, this wasn't done by lightning, this was done by an electrical circuit — the smaller holes by a 20-amp circuit, the bigger holes by a 40-amp circuit. You get an arc to this stuff, and a tenth of a coulomb — they characterize the strength of a lightning strike in coulombs. An ampere is a coulomb per second, so a coulomb is about 6 times 10 to the 19th electrons. A typical lightning strike is three to five coulombs of charge.

◆
§6.p2

They can go up to 400 coulombs in a really big strike, but that's pretty rare. They easily can go up to 80 coulombs in a big strike. This stuff can sustain a tenth of a coulomb without perforation. So what do you think the odds are of getting a hole in this if you get hit by lightning in your house? Pretty good.

◆
§6.p3

The industry acknowledges that by 2001 they knew about this, because they started doing research at the old General Electric Lightning Research Center in Pittsfield, Massachusetts. They used to build electrical distribution transformers for General Electric, and they had to deal with simulated lightning strikes, because it can get really exciting if lightning hits a distribution transformer and all the power goes out. So they have to protect these things from lightning — and I gave you the Ben Franklin article about lightning protection systems. They certainly learned by 2001; there's some evidence they started learning within the first couple of years. If you look at the National Fire Protection Association data, you see in 1998 a big jump in the number of fires due to lightning from fuel gas in homes.

◆
§6.p4

There are somewhere between half a million and a million of the 65 million homes in the United States that have this product in them. If it's in your home or a relative's home, get a lightning protection system. It'll only cost you four or five thousand dollars, and think of all the money you saved on the installation of your gas piping. So you can take the money you saved and now protect your home that won't burn down in case the lightning storm comes by.

◆
§6.p5

They realized they had a problem by 2003. A number of patents came out by different people; they decided they could improve things by making a conductive jacket. They basically added carbon black to the plastic, and now it looks like an automobile tire with carbon black in it. One of the products made by Gastite actually has two layers of plastic with a layer of perforated aluminum in between, and the Gastite product will sustain a lightning strike of 80 coulombs — 800 times safer because of that second layer of aluminum. There's another product made by a competitor with just the conductive plastic, and it will sustain about six coulombs.

◆
§6.p6

Six coulombs — if this is your median, and there are six coulombs — I'm not sure I'd want that in my house. I think I'd rather have this in my house. But if I really want to be safe I would have black iron pipe, because to my knowledge no one has ever heard of black iron pipe being perforated by lightning. Theoretically it's possible, but no one has heard of it.

◆
§6.p7

Why? You can go through the heat transfer. In the welding section I'll talk about the distance that heat travels in time being proportional to the square root of the thermal diffusivity times time, where α is thermal diffusivity. The thermal diffusivity of steel is a tenth of a centimeter squared per second. If we're talking about a circuit breaker, how fast will a circuit breaker close? 1/100th of a second, half of a 60-cycle. So it's 10 to the minus 2 seconds. I have the square root of 10 to the minus 3, which is 0.03. x equals 0.03 centimeters, which is 0.3 millimeters, about twelve thousandths of an inch.

◆
§6.p8

The CSST is ten thousandths of an inch, so it will blow through CSST in the typical time you might have, but it won't blow through black iron pipe, which is an eighth of an inch, ten times thicker. I simplified some of the heat flow — I didn't solve the time-dependent partial differential equation, and the real solution is about 0.3, not 0.12 inches. It just so happens, the guy at University of Florida caught some triggered lightning — you stick an antenna up in the air in a lightning storm and try to attract a real lightning bolt — and he got a seven-coulomb strike, hit a piece of stainless steel. He was doing this for the Department of Energy for the nuclear waste isolation materials in Nevada. They wanted to make sure that one of these three-inch-thick stainless steel casks wouldn't be perforated by a piece of lightning. They probably spent ten million dollars proving this when a simple calculation would tell you there's no way you could do it. They have to prove it to everyone, because no one trusts them.

◆
§6.p9

The simple heat flow says you'll perforate this stuff. It's just too thin if it gets hit by any kind of reasonable lightning strike. Black iron pipe is thick enough that it won't be. There are actually codes for the thickness of lightning terminals and lightning rods, and they tell you a lightning rod should have about the cross-sectional area of a piece of black iron pipe. The CSST in stainless steel is five times too thin in cross-section to carry the current to ground.

§7. Blame, grounding, and the impedance argument [40:43]

§7.p1

So what happened? They started out with this code in the nineties. They got it approved by different state agencies — to install gas piping has the force of law, I'm trying to tie this back to codes and standards. They find out about this problem, and in the meantime they don't really tell anybody. Some people know, but they're sort of trying to hide it. Why are they trying to hide it? You can't hide it completely, because when people's homes start burning down they get upset, insurance companies come in, and they get concerned when they start seeing these holes and people seeing three-foot jets of fire shooting out of their gas piping burning down their house.

◆
§7.p2

So they start having lawsuits, and what do they blame it on? The plumber who installed it. Blame it on someone else. Not the fact that you engineered it wrong, or didn't even consider lightning to begin with — which they've admitted: "we tested for earthquake resistance, we tested for these other things, no, we didn't think about lightning." Well, that's the problem with a new product, you can't think of every new thing. But when you get slapped in the face with it, you don't try to deep-six it.

◆
§7.p3

Up through about 2007 they were laying this off on the plumbers, saying you didn't ground it. To give you some idea of the grounding — let's say I have a fireplace, and here are my logs, and I have my nice flame in here, and this is a gas fireplace, and I've got my tubing coming out here. Let's say I also have some electricity coming in, and it's grounded, because they have a little electrical igniter so you hit a button to turn on your fireplace. So it's got electricity in here, it's got gas in here, and you might ground it to this. Of course this is electrically connected to that. But no — they told you to ground it to their ground, and their ground means you have to ground it to one of their fittings. And they'll sell you these fittings.

◆
§7.p4
